1.8 The investigation conducted by SEBI revealed inter alia that  the members 
of BgSE including the Broker were invo lved in creation of abnormally high 
volumes in the scrip of HTL and had reso rted to circular trading with other 
members of BgSE, wherein the s hares of HTL were traded amongst 
themselves, thereby rigging up the price of the scrip of HTL by executing a 
number of trades, which appeared to be not genuine, for various clients 
introduced by the employees of HTL. 
 
2.0    APPOINTMENT OF ENQUIRY OFFICER 
          
2.1   On completion of  the investigat ion, SEBI appointed an Enquiry Officer, vide 
order dated May 28, 2003 ,  under regulat ion 5(1) of SEBI (Procedure for 
Holding Enquiry by Enquiry Office r and Imposing Penalty) Regulations, 
2002 (hereinafter referred to as the 200 2 Regulations) to enquire into the 
alleged irregular transactions made by the Broker in the scrip of HTL. 
 
2.2 A show cause notice was also issued to the Broker and it was alleged that 
the Broker had resorted to circular trading with other members of BgSE 
wherein the shares of HTL were traded amongst themselves and rigged up 
the prices of the said scrip by tr ades which were not genuine and created 
artificial price and volumes. It was also alleged that t he Broker had no 
knowledge of his clients and did not  meet them generally against the 
concept of ‘Know Your Client’ as per  SEBI Guidelines and the orders on

behalf of the clients were placed by one Shri Rakesh Karanpuria of HTL at 
successively higher rates.  
 
2.3 Pursuant to this, the Enquiry Office r had given the Broker a fair and 
reasonable opportunity to make his s ubmissions. The Broker inter alia 
submitted before the Enquiry Officer that the c lients belonging to Porwal 
family were from Udaipur  and as the said client s were from Udaipur, he 
did not meet them pers onally. The Broker furthe r submitted that the

3.0     CONSIDERATION OF ISSUES AND FINDINGS.  
 
3.1  Based upon the recommendation of the Enquiry Officer, a show cause 
notice dated November 22, 2004 was issued to the Broker under regulation 
13(2) of the 2002 Regulations, asking hi m to show cause as to why the 
penalty as considered appropriate s hould not be imposed upon him.  A 
copy of the Enquiry Report was also forwarded to the Broker with the said 
show cause notice. The said show cause notice was served on the Broker 
through BgSE. Though, sufficient time wa s given to the Broker to file his 
reply, he has failed to submit any repl y to the said show cause notice till 
date. In the circumstances, it is observed that the Broker is not interested in 
filing any reply and in availing the opportunity of hearing before SEBI.  
 
3.2 As the Enquiry Officer has not recor ded any specific findings in respect of 
the violation of the prov isions of FUTP Regulatio ns, I deal with the other 
alleged violations attributed to the Broker.  I note that the Broker had 
executed significant trades in the scrip of HTL during the period November 
16, 1999 to December 31,  2001 on his proprietary account as well as on 
behalf of his clients namely, Shri Muke sh Porwal, Shri Amit Porwal and 
Shri Pradeep Porwal. The said transac tions were admitted by the Broker 
in the course of proceedings before the Enquiry Officer. When the price of 
the scrip of HTL was very high dur ing the period November 16, 1999 to 
March 31, 2000, the Broker had placed orders in the scrip of HTL in his 
proprietary account and on behalf his clients which amounted to 8.80% of 
the total volume at BgSE. I also note that the Brok er has failed to adduce

any evidence to show that the client s had traded in any scrip other than 
HTL during the time when the Broker had executed trades for them.   
 
3.3 It is the basic requirem ent that a stock broker has to compulsorily ensure 
that his clients are personally known to him or has been introduced to him 
in order to satisfy that they are genuine. The very purpose of such practice 
is to enable the stock brokers to ev aluate the client before the broker 
takes up trading on behalf of his clients.  
 
3.4 In terms of SEBI Cir cular dated February 11, 1997, the stock brokers were 
advised to maintain a database [perta ining to ‘Know Your Client’ (KYC) 
norms/ guidelines] of their clients. SEBI, vide circular dated April 11, 1997, 
had once again advised the stock broker s to follow the circular dated 
February 11, 1997 and further advised that the brokers might seek 
additional information, if any, so as  to satisfy themselves about the 
antecedents of the client and that it would be the responsibility of the stock 
brokers to provide for clients’ details as and when need arises.  
 
3.5 I note that, one of the c lients namely, Shri Amit Porwal was a student at 
the time when the Broker was executing transactions on his behalf in the 
scrip of HTL. The Broker had started trading in the scrip of HTL on behalf 
of the said client on March 02, 2000 and thereafter, he had executed 
continuous transactions on behalf the said  client. This is evident from the 
table shown at para 1.7 above,  relating to the trade details of the clients.  
However, the KYC form was obtained only on March 12, 2000, much after 
the trading was commenced on behalf of the said client. It can be seen 
from the transaction details that, Shri Amit Porwal had bought 4,100 
shares of HTL through the Broker, before the KYC form was obtained by 
the Broker. The said c lient had bought a total of  7,900 shares of HTL 
between March 02, 2000 and March 27, 2000,  when the price of the scrip

was at its maximum. The purchase by the above client amounted to 
47.02% to the total buy pos ition of the Broker at  BgSE, during the period 
November 16, 1999 and March 31, 200 0. I note that the Broker had 
commenced trading in the scrip of HT L on behalf his clients, before 
obtaining the KYC form from the said clients.  
 
3.6 I also note that in the case of Shri Mukesh Porwal, the KYC form was 
obtained by the Broker on March 12, 2000, whereas he had started 
trading on behalf of the said client in the scrip of HTL from February 29, 
2000.  It can be seen from the table at para 1.7 above, that the Broker had 
bought 1,100 shares of HT L before March 12, 2000, on  behalf of the said 
client. The total purchase by the Br oker on behalf the said client during 
February 29, 2000 to March 24, 2000 wa s 4,100 shares of HTL, which 
amounted to 24.41% to the total buy position of the Broker during the 
period November 16, 1999 and March 31, 2000. It may be mentioned here 
that, when the Broker was executing su ch trades, the price of the scrip of 
HTL was very high. The Broker had al so failed to adduce any evidence to 
suggest that his clients had traded in any other scrip. This view was 
further supported by the statement of Shri Mahendra Lunavat, Proprietor 
of the Broker made befor e the Investigating Officer of SEBI.  The Broker 
could have taken more care while dealing with such clients, who were not 
even introduced to him before the trades  and at a time when the price of 
the scrip of HTL was high. I note t hat the Broker had commenced trading 
in the scrip of HTL on behalf his cli ents, before obtaining the KYC form 
from the said clients.  
 
3.7 In view of the above, I note that t he Broker did not ac t in good faith and 
without negligence and did not exercise proper caution and diligence while 
dealing in the scrip of HTL on behalf of his clients.  In view of the above, it 
is established that t he Broker had failed to comply with the KYC norms

and verification of his clients, as requi red in terms of SEBI circulars dated 
February 11, 1997 and April 11, 1997 and thereby, violated the above 
circulars, pertaining to KYC norms/guidelines. 
 
3.8 It is one of the precaut ionary measures of a stoc k broker to verify the 
financial capacity of his clients before executing the trades on their behalf, 
which is one of the checks under the KYC norms/guidelines. Such 
assessment of financial capacity of the client is necessary in order to 
avoid the risk. When a stock broker fails to perform the said primary 
requirements and further, if he happens  to be transacting on behalf of 
such clients without knowing their ant ecedents and financial capacity, he 
is putting the entire system in jeopardy.   
 
3.9 I note that, one of the clients, Shri  Amit Porwal was a student when the 
Broker was trading in the scrip of HTL on his behalf. The annual income of 
the said client as reported in t he KYC form was only  Rs.40,000/-, 
however, the said client started trading in the scrip  of HTL from March 02, 
2000 and bought  7,900 shares till Marc h 27, 2000 ,when the price of the 
said scrip of HTL was at its maximum.   The said client  had also executed 
trades for 500 shares of HTL, duri ng the period April 12, 2000 and April 
27, 2000. I observe that the value of 200 shares of HTL bought by the said 
client , through the Broker, on  Ap ril 12, 2000 was  Rs.1,68,080/-.   
Further, the Broker had purchased 300 shares of HTL and sold 100 
shares of HTL on April 27, 2000, in volving Rs.2,53,960/- and Rs.85,000/- 
respectively. The net payment in the said transactions involved 
Rs.1,68,960/- to the Broker.   
 
3.10 I further note that, the annual inco me of Shri Pradip Porwal and Shri 
Mukesh Porwal in terms of the ‘KYC’ form was as Rs.55,000/- and 
Rs.60,000/-,  respectively. It can be seen that Shri Pradeep Porwal had

3.11 It can be seen that the annu al income of the afores aid clients as reported 
in the KYC form was substantially le ss as compared to the value of the 
transactions made by them. This will sh ow that the Broker had failed to 
assess the financial capacity of his clie nts, which is one of the important 
checks under the KYC norms.  The financia l capability of the client is an 
important factor to be ta ken into account by the broker before undertaking 
trading  on behalf of his clients and such assessment  is relevant even for 
the transactions which were squared off.  
 
3.12 As observed by the Hon’ble SAT in Madhukar Sheth  Vs.SEBI (Appeal 
No.46/2002), before ex ecuting series of transactions for his  client, any 
prudent broker would have gone a bi t far to ascertain the financial 
capability of the person for whom he was trading.  The transactions 
executed by the Broker appears to be excessive speculation to create 
artificial volumes in the scrip by himself and relatively new clients.   
 
3.13 The Enquiry Officer in his report had referred to the admission of the 
Broker that the securities were deliv ered personally to Shri Padam Porwal 
/ Shri Rakesh Karanpuria by hand delivery to be handed it over to the 
respective clients.  This is furthe r corroborated by the statement of Shri 
Rakesh Karanpuria, of HTL made on January 27, 2003 before the 
investigating officer that he had intr oduced the above clients to the Broker 
and used to place the orders and ta ke deliveries on behalf of the above 
clients. The Enquiry Officer has s pecified the above admission of the

Broker in his report, and concluded t hat the securities have been reached 
to the clients. However, I note that there is nothing on record  to show that 
the Broker had  received specific inst ructions or authorization from his 
clients to deliver the same to the aforesaid people.   
 
3.14 It is also noted that during the pe riod from November 16, 1999 to March 
31, 2001, the Broker had purchased about  1,45,000 shares of HTL from 
his own account. The trades of the Broker had constituted significant 
volumes at BgSE. It is observed that  maximum trades executed by the 
Broker were in t he proprietary account and most  of the said transactions 
were squared off and did not result in de liveries. It is also admitted by the 
Broker, during the investigations t hat he did jobbing transactions for 
various other scrips also.  
 
3.15 In view of the above, I note that  the Broker had failed to obtain the 
required details of his c lients, failed to assess th e financial capacity of his 
clients and not carried out the verifi cation as per  KYC norms/ guidelines, 
in terms of the above circulars, befor e executing the transactions on their 
behalf. Further, I note that the Brok er had dealt with Shri Rakesh 
Karanpuria without specific instruction or authorization.  
 
3.16 The Enquiry Officer has further observed that non obtaining the 
acknowledgement copies of the contract notes might be treated as a minor 
procedural lapse  in violation of regulation 17(1)(i)  of the Broker 
Regulations. In terms of the provisions  of the Broker R egulations, a stock 
broker, without any delay shall issue t he contract note to his client and in 
terms of regulation 17(1) (i) of the Broker Regulat ions, he has to maintain 
the counterfoils or duplicates of such contract notes.

3.17 In the present matter, the Broker had failed to submit even a single copy of 
acknowledgment of counter foils of contract notes. It can be seen from the 
trading details, that the Broker had executed several transactions of 
significant volume in the scrip of HTL on behalf his clients, without any 
specific authorization. De spite of executing large transactions, the Broker 
had failed to obtain the acknowledgement copy of contract notes of even in 
a single transaction. It does not appear to be a simple omission and has to 
be viewed seriously in the background of the allegations  that the orders 
were placed by Shri Rakesh Kar anpuria of HTL and the delivery of the 
securities were also  received by the same person.  I also note that the 
Broker had also traded subs tantially in the scrip of  HTL in his proprietary 
account during the period in November 16, 1999 and December 31, 2001 . 
 
3.18 As the Broker could not establish that he had verified the financial capacity 
of his clients and coupled with the admi tted facts that these clients were 
introduced by a third party before complying with KYC norms and who 
dealt only in the scrip of HTL will estab lish that the Broker  did not act in 
good faith and without negligence and did not exercise proper caution and 
diligence while trading in the scrip  of HTL by undertaking trade on behalf 
clients without KYC verification and t hereby,  failed to comply with the 
norms and guidelines relating to the KYC norms as per the SEBI circulars 
dated February 11, 1997 and April 11, 1997 and thereby vi olated the said 
circulars.  I also note that the Broker had made s ubstantial trading in the 
scrip of HTL on his proprietary account during the period between 
November 16, 1999 and December 31, 2001.  However, as there is no 
sufficient evidence to suggest that the Broker had violated the provisions 
of FUTP Regulations, I decide to reduce the quantum of penalty 
recommended  by the Enquiry Officer.
